# Early Estimation of Language to Latent Alignment in Diffusion Models (NoisyCLIP)

arXiv 2512.08505v2, read from the full PDF (31 pages, appendix included). The released code at `code/noisyclip/` (README and the four files in `src/`) was read and not run. The README names the venue as ECCV 2026; the PDF prints no venue.

## What it does

NoisyCLIP fine-tunes the image encoder of a CLIP-style model on half-denoised SDXL latents, so it can score how well a latent matches a prompt during sampling (p. 2, p. 6). The score ranks several seeds part way through generation and the weak seeds are stopped early, which the paper calls early-stopping Best-of-N (p. 7, p. 11). No gradient from the score enters the sampler anywhere in the paper.

## What the model or score reads

The image tower reads a noisy SDXL latent $z_t$ after a transformation $\Phi$ into an RGB-like image (p. 5). For SDXL, $\Phi$ is a linear transformation from the four latent channels to RGB, with no VAE decode (p. 8 to 9). For the SD3.5 and FLUX.1 tests, $\Phi$ is the model's VAE decoder instead (p. 9, p. 13).

The text tower is the CLIP text encoder, frozen (p. 6). The main results use ViT-L/14 (p. 8, p. 14, p. 28). The model receives no timestep or noise level as an input (`train_model_classic.py:14-20`).

## What it trained on

**Data.** Images and latents generated by SDXL from a rewritten version of CC12M captions (p. 5). The paper fine-tunes on 50,000 latents "uniformly sampled from the generated images" (p. 8).

**Noise levels.** Training uses a fixed window, latents 20 to 29 of a 50-step run, counted from the start of generation (p. 6, p. 11). Windows 10-19, 30-39 and 40-49 were also tried (p. 10 to 11).

**Pairs and loss.** Each latent is a positive pair with its own prompt, and every other pair in the batch is a negative, under InfoNCE (p. 6). The wrong captions described below are used for testing only.

**Training length.** 10 epochs, batch size 128, learning rate 5.7e-5, cosine schedule with warm-up ratio 0.1, weight decay 0.1 (p. 8).

## How its score or gradient enters

No gradient enters a sampler. The score is a dot product of the text and image embeddings, printed for the final image and for a latent (p. 5):

$$
S_{\text{final}}(x, y) = \tau_\theta(y) \cdot \nu_\theta(x)
$$

$$
S_{\text{latent}}(z_t, y) = \tau_\theta(y) \cdot \nu_\theta(\Phi(z_t))
$$

$\tau_\theta$ is the text encoder, $\nu_\theta$ the image encoder, $y$ the prompt, $x$ the final image, $z_t$ the latent and $\Phi$ the map into RGB-like space (p. 5). The score is used to pick one seed to continue. If two seeds run to latent 25 and one continues, the cost is $(25\times2)+(25\times1)=75$ steps against 100 for a full best-of-2 (p. 11).

## When adding scores is valid

This paper does not address it.

## Which of the five properties it bears on

**Counting.** The wrong-count caption is the wrong caption most often ranked first at every latent, at about 0.40 at latent 0 and 0.23 at latent 49 (Fig. 10, p. 23, read from the figure). The authors attribute this to CLIP's known weakness at counting (p. 23). When ranking 10 images on GenAI-Bench counting prompts at latents 21-30, VQAScore is 0.738 for NoisyCLIP, 0.716 for frozen CLIP and 0.717 for the candidate mean (Table 3, p. 25).

**Attribute binding.** Not tested as binding. The colour corruption replaces the colour of the main subject and never swaps colours between two objects (p. 22). The GenAI-Bench attribute score is 0.831 against 0.819 for frozen CLIP (Table 2, p. 24). The paper names a failure where the model swaps attributes of different elements (p. 29): for "A red bicycle against a blue wall", NoisyCLIP picks a blue bicycle against a red wall (Fig. 11, p. 30, read from the figure).

**Spatial relation.** Tested only by ranking images on GenAI-Bench spatial prompts: VQAScore 0.817 for NoisyCLIP, 0.805 for frozen CLIP, 0.798 for the candidate mean and 0.837 for frozen CLIP on the final image (Table 2, p. 24). No wrong caption swaps a relation or the order of two objects (p. 22).

**Position and size.** Not tested as a separate measure. The text says prompts with "quantity or size" improve, using the counting column (p. 25), and one qualitative example involves plant heights (p. 12 to 13).

**Prompt match.** This is the paper's main measure. Recall@1 for choosing the right caption among five, over latents 21-30, is 0.506 for NoisyCLIP and 0.145 for frozen CLIP (Table 1, p. 14).

## Unseen concepts

This paper does not test held-out concepts. It runs the SDXL-trained model on SD3.5 and FLUX.1 without retraining and reports alignment gains of 2% and 1.8% (p. 13 to 14). Its caption test set comes from the same CC12M source as its training captions (p. 5, p. 6, p. 8), and the paper does not state that the 1,000 test captions were excluded from training.

## What the released code does

**How latents are generated.** `generate_latents.py` loads SDXL base 1.0 (lines 9, 33) and calls the pipeline with only the prompt, generator and `eta` (line 79). The installed diffusers 0.43.0 source gives that call 50 steps and guidance 5.0 (`pipeline_stable_diffusion_xl.py:831,835`). A forward hook saves `input[0]` on every U-Net call (lines 41-42, 76), so each image gets 50 tensors. Index 0 is the first and noisiest U-Net input. Index 49 is the last U-Net input, and the latent after the final step is never saved. Each tensor holds the two identical classifier-free guidance copies (`pipeline_stable_diffusion_xl.py:1204`).

**Sampler.** The code does not swap the scheduler, so it uses the SDXL repository's default, which is Euler discrete (inferred, since the model's scheduler config was not opened). Euler's `step` has no `eta` argument and the pipeline drops it (`scheduling_euler_discrete.py`, `pipeline_stable_diffusion_xl.py:601-603`). The `--ddpm` flag therefore changes the output folder name and leaves the sampler unchanged (`generate_latents.py:60-61, 79`) (inferred).

**Which latent scale is saved.** The saved tensor is the scheduler-scaled U-Net input, $z_t / \sqrt{\sigma_t^2 + 1}$ (`pipeline_stable_diffusion_xl.py:1205`, `scheduling_euler_discrete.py:345`). The VAE scaling factor is not undone, because the pipeline divides by it only just before the VAE decode (`pipeline_stable_diffusion_xl.py:1285-1287`). A raw Euler latent, with a standard deviation near $\sigma_t$, is on a different scale and must be divided by $\sqrt{\sigma_t^2+1}$ before the map below (inferred).

**The input path into CLIP.** `latent_to_image.py:9-20` applies fixed weights and offsets:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}R\\G\\B\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix}60&-60&25&-70\\60&-5&15&-50\\60&10&-5&-35\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}z_0\\z_1\\z_2\\z_3\end{bmatrix}+
\begin{bmatrix}150\\140\\130\end{bmatrix}
$$

The result is clamped to 0 to 255, converted to 8-bit with `.byte()`, and saved as a PNG at latent resolution (line 18, and line 66). The default SDXL latent is 128 by 128 (inferred from the 1024-pixel default).

**Resize and normalisation are not in the released code.** `train_model_classic.py` loads ready-made `pixel_values` from a preprocessed dataset (lines 16, 180-181). `AutoProcessor` is imported and never used (line 4). The interpolation, crop and normalisation constants must be taken from the published checkpoint's processor config.

**The gradient path.** `.byte()` has no gradient and clamped pixels have zero gradient (line 18). A push through this model needs a rewritten map that drops `.byte()` (inferred). For a unit-variance latent, the clamp saturates about 26.8% of red, 11.4% of green and 7.0% of blue pixels (computed from the weights above, assuming independent zero-mean channels; inferred).

**What is frozen.** The `--only_vision` flag sets `requires_grad = not ("text" in name)` (`train_model_classic.py:48-49`). This freezes `text_model` and `text_projection`. It leaves `vision_model` and `visual_projection` trainable, and freezes `logit_scale` (lines 50-51, 71). No released flag trains the vision tower while freezing `visual_projection`, and the command used for the published checkpoint is not released.

**What the published checkpoint holds.** Comparing `asiimo/noisyclip` with `openai/clip-vit-large-patch14` tensor by tensor ([the comparison output](tools/checkpoint-compare.txt)) shows `visual_projection.weight`, `text_projection.weight` and the first text encoder layer's key projection are identical, with a largest absolute difference of 0. The first vision encoder layer's key projection differs by 6.1% of its norm, so the vision tower was tuned. `logit_scale` is 4.59375 against 4.60517, a score multiplier of 98.87 against 100.00. The other text layers were not compared.

**The loss.** The collate function sets `return_loss: True` (line 19). Hugging Face `CLIPModel` then returns the mean of the image-to-text and text-to-image cross entropies over the batch similarity matrix (`modeling_clip.py:47-54, 822-823` in the repository's copy). The negatives are the other items in the batch.

**Noise levels sampled.** The released code contains no step selection and adds no noise during training. The 20-29 window exists only in the paper (p. 6) and in whatever preprocessed dataset was built from it.

**Where code and paper disagree, or the paper disagrees with itself**

- The paper trains with learning rate 5.7e-5, weight decay 0.1, 10 epochs and batch 128 on ViT-L/14 (p. 8). The script defaults are 1e-4, 0.0, 5, 64 and `openai/clip-vit-base-patch32` (`train_model_classic.py:286-287, 304, 306, 314`). The flags used for the real run are not released.
- The paper says only the image encoder is tuned and never mentions projection heads (p. 6, p. 8). The released vision-only flag also trains `visual_projection` (`train_model_classic.py:49`), while the published checkpoint's `visual_projection` equals base CLIP's exactly.
- The paper calls $\Phi$ the "inverse of E" (p. 5). For SDXL it is a fixed linear approximation (p. 9; `latent_to_image.py:10-16`).
- `corrupt_prompts.py:119` calls `exit()` before generation, so the released script prints one prompt and stops.
- `train_model_classic.py:1` is a bare `python` line, which raises `NameError` if the file is run as released (inferred, not run).
- Table 4 and the text give frozen CLIP on basic prompts as 19.8% of the final score (p. 26, p. 27). Its own values give $0.183/0.296 = 61.8\%$.
- The text says frozen CLIP stays below 0.1 Recall@1 within the first 25 iterations (p. 10). Fig. 4 shows it near 0.13 at latent 25 (read from the figure).
- The text says frozen CLIP separates images "by latent 40" (p. 9) and that its gap "only emerges after latent 20" (p. 10).
- The text says the method "remains superior" on advanced prompts (p. 26). On negation it scores 0.517, below frozen CLIP at 0.522 and the candidate mean at 0.523 (Table 3, p. 25).
- The text reports "+5% over baseline" for 0.709 against 0.657 (p. 14). That is 5.2 points, or 7.9% in relative terms.

## How it makes wrong captions, and results per corruption

**How the wrong captions are made.** Gemma 3 27B rewrites each original caption four times, changing one aspect each time and keeping the sentence structure (p. 22). The released script uses a 4-bit quantised copy of that model, temperature 0.8 and top-p 0.95 (`corrupt_prompts.py:49-53, 105`). The test has 1,000 captions, four generated images per caption and five candidate captions per image (p. 8, p. 10).

| Corruption | What changes | Paper's example (p. 22) |
|---|---|---|
| Color | colour of the primary subject | "a red apple" to "a green apple" |
| Count | number of the principal subject | "two dogs" to "three dogs" |
| Background | setting of the main subject | "a cat on a mat" to "a cat on a table" |
| Main subject | the principal subject itself | "a bird on a branch" to "a squirrel on a branch" |

None of the four keeps the same words in a new order. This loop's pairs, "a cat to the left of a dog" against "a dog to the left of a cat" and "a red cat and a blue dog" against "a blue cat and a red dog", are therefore a harder test than anything in this benchmark (inferred).

## What to copy, what to avoid

- Copy the frozen text tower, so the right and wrong sentence embeddings stay fixed and the score difference changes only through the latent (inferred).
- Copy the fixed linear map, rewritten without `.byte()`, and feed it the scheduler-scaled latent $z_t/\sqrt{\sigma_t^2+1}$. Feeding a raw Euler latent would saturate almost every pixel at early steps (inferred).
- Before trusting a gradient, read the processor config from the checkpoint, since the resize and normalisation are absent from the released code (inferred).
- Report right-against-wrong pairwise accuracy at steps 0, 5, 10 and 15, with the same sentence pair scored on pure noise as a baseline. The Fig. 10 rates at latent 0 show that a sentence can win before any image content exists (inferred).
- Avoid expecting a useful push at steps 0 to 9 from the stock checkpoint, because the right caption ranks first less often than chance there (Fig. 4, p. 10). A retrain for this loop's early window should include latents before 20: the 10-19 model scores 0.662 at latents 0-10 against 0.648 for the 20-29 model (Fig. 5, p. 10) (inferred).
- Avoid treating this paper as evidence that the model reads word order or binding. Its benchmark has no swap captions, and its named failure case is an attribute swap (p. 22, p. 29 to 30) (inferred).
- Treat the gradient as untested. The paper only ranks latents, so the earlier CLIP result, where the checker raised its own score by 1 to 13.5 logits over a random push without moving the animals, remains the first risk. Keep the random-push control (inferred).
- Expect counting to be a weak sentence pair, since the wrong-count caption is the strongest distractor at every latent (Fig. 10, p. 23) (inferred).

## Closer work it cites

- **Controlling Latent Diffusion Using Latent CLIP**, arXiv 2503.08455 (p. 15, reference 3). A CLIP model that reads latents directly, trained on clean $z_0$ latents (p. 3, p. 12). Its title says it controls latent diffusion, so it is the nearest prior work on steering with a latent text-image score.
- **Dynamic Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance via Online Feedback**, arXiv 2509.16131 (p. 18, reference 32). The paper describes it as using latent information to choose classifier-free guidance during reverse diffusion (p. 3).
